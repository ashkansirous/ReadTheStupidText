Privacy Policy — Dino Book Reader

Effective date: 9 November 2026

Dino Book Reader (“the app”) is a book reader for children aged 3 to 9. It reads stories aloud with a different voice for each character, and a family can record their own voice for the characters. This policy explains how the app handles data, for the children who use it and the grown-ups who set it up.

The short version

The Dino Book Reader app does not ask for, collect or share personal information such as names, accounts, photos or recordings. Every request it makes carries the device’s IP address, as all internet traffic does; the only place this project’s server keeps that address is a short technical request log that Cloudflare keeps and deletes on its own schedule (see “Server request log” below). It has no accounts, no advertising, and no third-party analytics. It does send anonymous usage counts to this project’s own server, with no name, no identifier of the child, the install or the device, and nothing a child typed; a grown-up can turn this off in Settings (see “Anonymous usage counts” below). Like most web servers, this project’s server keeps a short technical log of requests, which can include the IP address, for as long as Cloudflare sets (see “Server request log” below). If the app crashes it can also send a crash report on the same switch (see “Crash reports” below). Everything else the app does happens on the device, apart from downloading free stories and their audio from this project’s own server. Your family’s recordings are never sent.

What the app processes, and where

  • Stories come only from this project’s free story store. The app has no way to add a book from typed text, files, photos or scans.
  • Speech is the stories’ pre-made audio, downloaded from the store, plus a family’s own recordings. The app does not turn text into speech on the device, and no text is sent to any server to be read aloud or translated. Translations are built into the stories.
  • Recordings of a family’s voice are made only if a grown-up opens the Record screen (behind the grown-ups gate) and taps record, to read a character’s lines in their own voice. A recording is kept for one story language of one story. It is saved on the device, in that story’s private folder, and is played back when the story is read. It is never sent to anyone, never analysed and never used for anything else. It is included in the app’s own backup file only when a grown-up makes one and chooses where to keep it (see the backup note below), and it is deleted when the grown-up deletes it or the story. On Android the app turns off the system’s automatic app backup. On iPhone the app excludes only its downloaded audio-pack folder from the device’s own iCloud or computer backup; its other files, including a family’s recordings (kept in the app’s own data folder with the stories), are not excluded, so if the family uses that backup, it may include them; the app itself never uploads them. The app does not record the child, and does not listen at any other time.
  • Settings and the story library (the story languages, reading progress, display choices) are stored on the device and are not uploaded; the only facts about them that leave it are the few anonymous counts listed under “Anonymous usage counts” (such as which story languages were chosen).
  • Backup and restore. A grown-up can save one backup file of the stories and recordings, and choose where it is kept; the app does not upload it. Restoring reads a file the grown-up picks.
  • The app contains no advertising and no in-app purchases, and it does not link children to other apps or websites.

Network use

Free stories and their audio. The app shows a store of free stories (a new install picks its first books from it, and a grown-up can open it again behind the grown-ups gate). Opening it fetches the list of stories, their cover pictures, and any story chosen, from this project’s own server (dinoreader.sirous.uk). The app also downloads each book’s pre-made audio, one pack per language the family has chosen, from the same server (the addresses come from that list), and now and then (when the app starts, when it comes back to the front, when the story language changes, and in a weekly update check) fetches the list again to check for a newer version of a book on the shelf, and downloads the newer text or audio. These are plain file requests: no account, no identifier, and nothing about the child or the recordings is sent. The app does not use cookies. Each time the app starts or comes back to the front it also fetches a small settings file (dinoreader.sirous.uk/api/app-config: whether counting is on, and the oldest app version still allowed), again a plain request that sends nothing about the child. Once a book and its audio are on the device the app works offline. The server’s own short request log is described under “Server request log” below. What the app counts about reading is described next.

Anonymous usage counts. To learn which books families enjoy and which features are used, the app counts a few things and sends the counts to this project’s own server (dinoreader.sirous.uk), in one small batch when a reading session ends or the app next starts. Today it counts: that a book was opened and that a book was read to the end; that a book was listened to for 30 seconds or more, and how many seconds of audio were played in a reading (one whole number of seconds when the reading is paused or stopped); that a free story was downloaded (and whether the age or author filter was in use, never which); that a reading started, was paused, was resumed or stopped (and, from a fixed list, why it stopped: the book was left, it reached the end, it was cancelled, the language changed, a new reading replaced it, it was skipped, it failed or the app stopped it for another internal reason), and that a book was left; that a book on the shelf was checked against a newer version in the store, and the two version numbers; that a family recording was made (and roughly how many pages of the book are recorded in that language: 1, 2–5, 6–10 or 11 or more); that the app language was changed (from which language to which); that a “please wait” panel appeared or was cancelled (which kind of wait, from a fixed list, and for how long, as a coarse bucket); that the first-run introduction was started (the first time or as a replay), which of its steps (finding the places, a first read, recording, choosing story languages) was tried or skipped, and whether it was finished or skipped; that a grown-up chose which languages stories can be in (how many, from one to three, and where it was set, and each language added); that a child switched a story’s language while reading (to which language); which audio a reading used (the book’s pre-made audio for every sentence, for some of them, or for none, with how many sentences each way and, from a fixed list, why some had none); that a download of a book’s pre-made audio started, finished, was cancelled or failed (and, from a fixed list, how it failed); that a feature fell back to something simpler (such as an audio clip that could not be played or the store that could not be reached; the part of the app and the reason come from fixed lists, with how many times it happened in that session); and that the app was opened. Each count carries only: what happened; which free store book it was (its public story code), never its title or text; the language; the one extra fact named above (such as the page range or a version number); whether a family recording is on the book; the app version; the platform (Android, iPhone or Windows); and, added by the server, the time the batch arrived and the random session number described below.

Each batch of counts also says a few coarse things about the device, as fixed lists and ranges: the form factor (phone, tablet, desktop or other), the operating system’s major version, the processor type (arm64, x64, x86, arm or other), the number of processor cores as a range (1 to 4, 5 to 8, or 9 or more) and the memory as a range in GB (under 3, 3 to 4, 4 to 6, 6 to 8, or 8 or more). Never the device’s model or maker, the exact chip, exact memory or core numbers, the full system build, or any identifier. The server also records the country of the request as a two-letter code (such as GB), which Cloudflare works out from the connection. It is approximate, not a location: the app never asks for location permission and never sends one, and the IP address is not stored with the counts (it can appear in the short server request log described below). These are coarse ranges, not exact values, and nothing here is a name, an account, a device model or an identifier. They are not a guarantee of anonymity on their own, though: in a small country a rare combination of ranges could stand out, so they are used only to see the overall picture.

Each count also says four more coarse things, from fixed lists: the language the app’s screens are in (one of the fifteen the app offers), whether this is the very first time the app was opened or a later time, as a range (first time, the same day, 1 to 7 days later, 8 to 30 days later, or more than 30 days later), how many times the app has been opened, as a range (once, 2 to 5, 6 to 20, or more than 20), and how many seconds have passed since the app was opened this time. The app works out the first two ranges on the device from the day it was first opened and a count of openings, which stay on the device and are never sent; only the range is sent. This is not an install identifier: two installs in the same range cannot be told apart.

There is no name, account, advertising or device identifier, no install identifier, no IP address kept with the counts (see “Server request log” below), and nothing a child typed, so a count carries nothing that names a child, a family or a device. The one number that goes with the counts is a random session number: it is made new every time the app starts and is never reused for a later start. It lives in memory, and is also written only inside counts still waiting in the app's local send queue; it is removed from the queue once those counts are sent. The server stores it with each count, and a crash report or a Report a problem from that start of the app carries it too, so the reports and the counts of one start of the app can be put side by side. It is not tied to a person, an install or a device, and it cannot tell two sessions of one person apart. It puts the counts of one start of the app in order. The server (like any web server) may see the IP address of the request, which is not stored with the counts (only the approximate country it maps to, as described above); it can appear in the short server request log described below. The counts are stored with Cloudflare Analytics Engine (our processor), which keeps them for the time Cloudflare sets for that service (Cloudflare documents three months), and read only as totals. Once an hour the project also copies daily totals (a day, a book, a platform and a count, with no session number) into its own database, and keeps them there. The project can turn counting down or off from the server without an update. A grown-up can switch it off at any time under “Help make Dino better” in Settings (behind the grown-ups gate); when it is off, nothing is sent. Development builds, emulators and the test devices store review tools use send no counts. Builds installed through TestFlight, and the copy Apple’s App Review opens, send the same counts to a separate store for beta builds; they are never added to the counts of the published app.

Crash reports (Android and iPhone). If the app crashes or stops unexpectedly, it can send a crash report to this project’s own server (dinoreader.sirous.uk) the next time it starts. It follows the same switch as the counts: a grown-up can turn it off under “Help make Dino better” in Settings (behind the grown-ups gate), and then nothing is kept or sent. A report holds: the app and its version; the platform; whether the app came from the store or from TestFlight; the kind of failure; the error’s type, message and call stack (file paths are removed from messages); the last few hundred lines of the app’s technical log (32 KB at most), which hold technical events and error details, which can include the names of audio clip files, the codes of books and where in a book the reading was, but never a story’s text, a recording, anything a child typed or a picture; the time to the minute (UTC); and the same coarse device ranges as the counts. The phone also passes on crashes and hangs that the system itself records for the app (Apple’s MetricKit on iPhone, Android’s process exit reasons). It has no name, account, advertising, install or device identifier; the server gives each report a random name and does not store the IP address with the report (it only reads the approximate country, and its rate limiter uses the address briefly to stop one connection sending too many reports; the upload request itself appears in the short server request log described below). Reports are kept with Cloudflare (our processor), read only by the project to find and fix bugs, and are not shared or used for advertising or tracking. Test and development builds and the test devices store review tools use send none; builds installed through TestFlight do.

Report a problem (Android and iPhone). In Settings, behind the grown-ups gate, there is a Report a problem page. Nothing is sent until a grown-up presses Send on it, whatever the counts switch says. The report holds the same things as a crash report without an error: the app and its version, the platform, whether the app came from the store or from TestFlight, the app’s recent technical log (32 KB at most; the app’s recent technical log lines, which may include lines from earlier sessions and can include the names of audio clip files, the codes of books and where in a book the reading was, but never a name, a story’s text, a recording, anything a child typed or a picture), the time to the minute (UTC) and the same coarse device ranges and country as the counts. It also holds a random 8-character code that the app shows after sending, so a grown-up can quote it when writing to us; the code names that one report and nothing else (not the child, the phone or the install). It goes to this project’s own server (dinoreader.sirous.uk), is kept with Cloudflare (our processor), is read only by the project to find and fix bugs, is deleted automatically after 90 days (as crash reports are), and is not shared or used for advertising or tracking.

On Windows the app sends the anonymous counts described above, on the same switch, but it sends no crash reports and has no Report a problem page.

No other services. The app contains no Google ML Kit, no advertising or analytics software from any other company, and makes no request to any server other than this project’s own (dinoreader.sirous.uk). Like any web request, a download or a batch of counts is seen by the server that receives it, which may log the device’s IP address; this project’s own server keeps only the short request log described next. When a store listing or an app update is opened, the app store (Google Play, the App Store or the Microsoft Store) handles that under its own privacy policy.

Server request log

This project’s own server (dinoreader.sirous.uk) runs on Cloudflare Workers, and Cloudflare, which acts as the project’s processor, keeps a short technical log of each request to it for the project, whatever the request is for (including a public page of usage totals, dinoreader.sirous.uk/stats, which shows only totals). Read The Stupid Text’s server (readthestupidtext.sirous.uk) works the same way. A log entry holds the address that was asked for (including anything after a “?” in it), the time, the status of the server’s answer, the approximate country, and other request details Cloudflare records, which can include the connecting IP address. The log also holds the server’s own short error and status messages. This is Cloudflare’s own short log, kept for as long as Cloudflare sets for it; the length is not set by this project, the app or the counts. The project uses it only to find and fix problems with the server and does not combine it with the usage counts or crash reports; it is not shared and never used for advertising or tracking.

Permissions

The app declares the Internet and Network state permissions, used only to download free stories and their audio, send the counts and reports described above, and to tell “offline” from a failed download. On Android it also declares the Microphone permission (on iPhone and Windows the matching microphone access), asked for only when a grown-up first taps record on the Record screen, and used only while a recording is running. On Android the app also declares the Foreground service (data sync) and Notifications permissions, because it includes a shared download component that can keep a large download running with a progress notice that has a Cancel button, and a “download paused” notice. This app downloads no such packs, so nothing starts that component; the permissions send nothing. The app’s code never asks for camera, photo, contacts or location access. The iPhone build still declares camera and photo-library usage texts that nothing uses; they are being removed. Saving or restoring a backup uses the standard file picker or share sheet of the device, and the app only opens the file a grown-up picks.

Children’s privacy

Dino Book Reader is designed for children, and the app does not ask for, collect or share personal information about anyone, including children: no name, email address, photos or recordings. It has no accounts, no ads, no social features, no behavioural tracking, and no way for a child to send information to anyone. The only data that leaves the device is the anonymous counts and crash reports described above (no identifier, off at a grown-up’s choice), and a Report a problem that a grown-up chooses to send, none of which includes any content a child creates. Settings and the story library sit behind a grown-ups gate. Because the app collects no personal information, there is nothing for a parent to review, correct or delete beyond the content on their own device, which they can remove by deleting a story or clearing the app’s storage. The only personal data this project’s server keeps is the connecting IP address in its short request log (see “Server request log” above); log entries hold no name or account, and Cloudflare deletes them on its own schedule.
